Literary style

Bir zamanlar

Turkish fiction uses the grammar you know in ways conversation rarely does. A story settles on a base tense and leaves it on purpose; long sentences are built from chains of -(y)Ip and -(y)ArAk and then cut short; older forms such as gidesim geldi and -cAsInA come back; words move after the verb for rhythm; and the narrator slips into a character's head without a single diye. This unit shows how to read those choices, and how to make a few of them yourself.

01Telling a story: -DI, -mIş and the historical present

A narrative has a base tense, and every move away from it means something. Three bases are common in Turkish prose.

Base tenses of narration
BaseEffectWhere you meet it
-DIthe narrator was there, or tells it as if they were; events follow one anotherrealist novels, memoirs, most modern fiction
-mIşdistance: the story was heard, not seenfolk tales (Bir varmış, bir yokmuş), legends, family history from before the narrator was born, dreamlike passages
-(I)yor, the historical presentthe scene happens before the reader's eyesa climax inside a -DI story; sometimes a whole story

Around the base, the other past tenses do their usual work: -(I)yordu sets the background and the action that gets interrupted (B1 unit 1), -mIştI reaches back to an earlier event (B1 unit 1), and -(I)rdI describes habits (B1 unit 2): Kasaba sabahları erken uyanırdı. The aorist without -DI gives description a timeless air: Kasabada herkes herkesi tanır.

The historical present. A story told in -DI can switch to the present at its turning point, so that the reader seems to be there: Kapıyı açtım. Karşımda kim var, biliyor musunuz? Storytellers often announce the switch with bir de ne göreyim! (and what do I see!) or derken (and just then). The switch back to -DI closes the scene.

Chains. One finite verb at the end of a sentence can carry the tense and the person for a whole series of actions joined with -(y)Ip (A2 unit 11), while -(y)ArAk adds how they were done (B1 unit 7). Prose builds long sentences this way, and then cuts them with a short one.

Examples
  • Kapıyı açtım. Karşımda kim var, biliyor musunuz?I opened the door. And who do you think is standing in front of me?
  • Bir varmış, bir yokmuş; evvel zaman içinde bir padişah varmış.Once upon a time, long ago, there was a sultan.
  • Annem o yıl İzmir'e taşınmış; ben henüz doğmamışım.That year, as I was told, my mother moved to Izmir; I hadn't been born yet.
  • Eve döndü, ceketini çıkarıp koltuğa oturdu ve gözlerini kapayarak bir süre öylece kaldı.She came home, took off her jacket and sat down in the armchair, and stayed like that for a while with her eyes closed.
  • Derken kapı çaldı.And just then, the doorbell rang.
Watch out

-(y)Ip takes no tense and no person: the final verb gives them to the whole chain. Çıkarıp oturdu is two past actions; dönüpler or döndüp do not exist.

Tip

Phrases that move a story on: derken (and then, just then), gel zaman git zaman (time went by, in tales), aradan yıllar geçti (years went by), bir de baktım ki (C1 unit 7).

02Older and literary forms: gidesi gelmek, -(y)ArAktAn, -cAsInA

Literature keeps forms that everyday Turkish uses less, or only in fixed phrases. You need to recognize them all, and you can use a few of them yourself.

To feel like doing: -(y)AsI with a possessive. The -(y)AsI form names something wished for or destined. With a possessive and gelmek, it says that a wish came over someone: gidesim geldi (I felt like going, literally my going came), ağlayası geldi (she felt like crying). The person is shown only by the possessive; gelmek stays in the third person. It sounds literary, or playful in speech; the neutral way to say it is gitmek istedim or canım gitmek istedi.

Negative, question and present
FormExampleMeaning
negativeHiç gidesim gelmedi.I didn't feel like going at all.
questionGidesin geldi mi?Did you feel like going?
presentGidesim geliyor.I feel like going.
present, negativeBugün hiç çalışasım gelmiyor.I don't feel like working at all today.
with var and yok, colloquialBugün hiç çalışasım yok.I'm in no mood to work today.

The same form survives in a few fixed words: olası (possible, C1 unit 2), and curses such as kahrolası (damned, literally may it be ruined). With the dative it makes adverbs of degree: doyasıya (to your heart's content, literally until you are full) and ölesiye (to death, deadly): ölesiye yorgun, dead tired.

-(y)ArAktAn. In folk songs, folk poetry and some regional speech, -(y)ArAk takes an extra -tAn: gülerekten, ağlayaraktan. Standard prose writes -(y)ArAk; read the long form as a mark of oral style.

-cAsInA (B2 unit 2) is at home in literature: kırarcasına (as if to break it), ölürcesine (as if dying), delicesine (madly). The first sentence of Aşk-ı Memnu has a boat passing çarparcasına, as if about to ram the women's boat (see the interlude).

idi, imiş, ise, iken as words. Older prose, and some writers today, write the copula as a separate word (A2 unit 2): Hava güzel idi for güzeldi, gelmiş idi for gelmişti, çocuk iken for çocukken. It slows the sentence down and gives it weight. Dek (until) and evvel (before, earlier) are the literary partners of kadar and önce: sabaha dek, evvel zaman içinde.

Examples
  • Birden ağlayası geldi.Suddenly she felt like crying.
  • Bu sabah hiç işe gidesim yoktu.I was in no mood to go to work this morning.
  • Bütün gece doyasıya konuştuk.We talked to our heart's content all night.
  • Ölesiye yorgundu, ama uyuyamadı.She was dead tired, but she couldn't sleep.
  • Çocuk, kapıyı kırarcasına çalıyordu.The child was knocking at the door as if to break it down.
  • Hava çok güzel idi; deniz sakin idi.The weather was very fine; the sea was calm.Today, güzeldi, sakindi.
Watch out

Only the possessive changes: gidesim geldi, gidesin geldi. Gidesim geldim is wrong: the wish is what comes, so gelmek stays in the third person.

Compare

French has the same picture of a wish that comes to you: gidesim geldi is close to l'envie m'a pris de partir, and doyasıya to tout son soûl.

03Inversion and rhythm

In conversation, what follows the verb is known background or an afterthought (C1 unit 6). Literature uses the same slot for rhythm and emphasis. A subject placed after the verb lingers at the end of the sentence: Uyuyordu kasaba holds the image of the town where the voice drops. A time or place after the verb stretches the moment: Yürüdük, sabaha kadar. A verb at the very start, in a short sentence, gives suddenness: Gitti. Dönmedi bir daha.

Rhythm. Prose also works with repetition (Bekledim, bekledim, bekledim; gelmedi.), with series of clauses of the same shape separated by semicolons, and with a long sentence whose meaning arrives only with its last word, followed by a short one. Turkish lends itself to the long sentence: everything that describes a noun comes before it (B1 unit 5), so the reader waits for the head. In this sentence from Aşk-ı Memnu, izdivaç demek (marriage meant) has to wait for yalı demekti (meant a waterside mansion).

Poetry. Metre and rhyme move words much further. Syllabic metre (hece ölçüsü) and the quantitative metre of divan poetry (aruz) decide where the stresses fall, and the rhyme word ends the line. To read an older couplet, put it back into prose order. Ziya Paşa's famous line puts the predicate iştir in the middle of the possessive phrase kişinin âyinesi:

The couplet in prose order
PoemProse orderNote
Âyinesi iştir kişininKişinin aynası işidir.âyine is the old word for ayna
lafa bakılmazLafa bakılmaz.an impersonal passive: one doesn't look at words
Şahsın görünür rütbe-i aklı eserindeŞahsın aklının rütbesi eserinde görünür.rütbe-i akıl is an izafet (C1 unit 4): the rank of the mind
Examples
  • Uyuyordu kasaba; yalnız denizin sesi duyuluyordu.The town was asleep; only the sound of the sea could be heard.
  • Gitti. Dönmedi bir daha.He left. He never came back.
  • Yürüdük, sabaha kadar.We walked, all the way to morning.
  • Bekledim, bekledim, bekledim; gelmedi.I waited and waited and waited; he didn't come.
  • Ne bir ses vardı ne bir ışık.There was neither a sound nor a light.A correlative (B2 unit 10) with the verb shared.
Watch out

Inversion belongs to narration, dialogue and poetry. Reports, essays and formal letters keep the verb at the end (C1 unit 6).

04Free indirect speech and the narrator's voice

Fiction has three ways of giving a character's thought. The third, free indirect speech (serbest dolaylı anlatım), keeps the narrator's grammar but lets the character's own words through.

Three ways of telling a thought
WayExampleWhose voice
directAyşe, “Yarın gidiyorum.” diye düşündü.the character's words in quotation marks, with diye
indirectAyşe ertesi gün gideceğini düşündü.the narrator reports: a noun clause and a verb of thinking
free indirectAyşe pencereden baktı. Yarın gidiyordu.the narrator's third person and past; the character's time words, questions and feelings

How to recognize it. The sentence is in the third person and in the past, usually -(I)yordu or -(y)AcAktI (B1 unit 2); yet it keeps the character's own yarın, şimdi and artık, asks the character's questions without diye (Ne yapacaktı şimdi?), exclaims (Ne aptallık!), and uses the particles of speech: ya, işte, hani, acaba, demek.

-mIş and irony. A narrator can repeat a character's claim in -mIş and keep a wry distance from it (B1 unit 12): Kardeşi yine geç kalmıştı. Çok önemli bir işi varmış! The exclamation mark tells you the narrator, or the sister, doesn't believe a word.

The narrator. Turkish criticism distinguishes the first-person narrator (ben anlatıcı), the all-knowing narrator with the omniscient point of view (hâkim bakış açısı) and the observer (gözlemci bakış açısı); bilinç akışı is stream of consciousness. The first Turkish novelists liked a narrator who stops the story to talk to the reader; by the end of the 19th century, Halit Ziya's narrator stays out of sight and slides into his characters' thoughts (see the interlude).

Examples
  • Ayşe pencereden baktı. Yarın gidiyordu. Bir daha bu sokağı görmeyecekti belki.Ayşe looked out of the window. She was leaving tomorrow. Perhaps she would never see this street again.
  • Ne yapacaktı şimdi? Kime söyleyecekti?What was she going to do now? Who was she going to tell?
  • Kardeşi yine geç kalmıştı. Çok önemli bir işi varmış!Her brother was late again. He had something very important to do, apparently!
  • Mektubu okudu, bir daha okudu. Demek her şey bitmişti.She read the letter, and read it again. So it was all over.
  • Hüzünlüydü; gurbette ilk bayramıydı bu.She felt sad: this was her first bayram far from home.
Tip

To spot free indirect speech, try adding diye düşündü after the sentence. If it fits, you are inside a character's head.

Compare

French readers know the device as style indirect libre, the voice of Flaubert's Madame Bovary; Turkish builds it the same way, from the narrator's past tense and the character's words.

Summary

  1. A story picks a base tense: -DI when the narrator was there, -mIş for tales and unwitnessed family history, the present for a vivid scene.
  2. The historical present marks a turning point, often after bir de ne göreyim! or derken.
  3. One finite verb ends a chain of -(y)Ip; -(y)ArAk adds manner; a short sentence cuts the long one.
  4. Gidesim geldi: -(y)AsI + possessive + gelmek is I felt like going; doyasıya, ölesiye, kahrolası keep the same form.
  5. Literary prose may write idi, imiş, ise, iken apart, and uses dek and evvel for kadar and önce.
  6. After the verb, literature puts what it wants to linger on: Uyuyordu kasaba. To read old poetry, put it back in prose order.
  7. Free indirect speech: third person and past, but the character's questions, time words and particles, with no diye.
