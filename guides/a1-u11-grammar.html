Numbers and time

Saat kaç?

Turkish numbers are regular: learn ten digits and a few round numbers, and you can build any number by putting the words in a row, yirmi beş (25), iki bin yirmi altı (2026). This unit also covers the singular noun after a number (iki kitap, two books), ordinals (birinci, first), the clock, dates, ages and prices. Several of them reuse the case endings you already know.

01Bir, iki, üç: cardinal numbers

The numbers from zero to ten, then the tens, a hundred and a thousand. These twenty-two words are all you need.

Building numbers. Put the parts in a row, the biggest first, each as a separate word: on bir (11), yirmi beş (25), yüz on (110), üç yüz kırk (340). Hundreds and thousands multiply: iki yüz (200), beş bin (5,000), on bin (10,000).

No bir before yüz and bin. A hundred is just yüz and a thousand just bin, never bir yüz or bir bin. A million, though, is bir milyon.

Suffixes on digits. A suffix on a number written in digits takes an apostrophe, as in unit 5, and follows the sound of the last word you say: 3'te (üçte, at three), 6'da (altıda), 10'a (ona), 1923'te (üçte), 2026'da (altıda).

Examples
  • Bu kitap üç yüz sayfa.This book is three hundred pages long.
  • Sınıfta yirmi iki öğrenci var.There are twenty-two students in the class.
  • İstanbul'da on beş milyon insan var.There are fifteen million people in Istanbul.
  • Saat 3'te geliyorum.I'm coming at three.
Watch out

Turkish writes a dot between thousands and a comma for decimals, the other way round from English: 1.000 is a thousand, and 2,5 is two and a half, said iki virgül beş.

Tip

In shops and cafés you'll often hear tane (piece, item) after a number: İki tane çay, lütfen (Two teas, please). It adds nothing to the meaning; İki çay, lütfen is just as good.

02İki kitap: the singular after numbers

After a number, the noun stays singular: iki kitap (two books), üç çocuk (three children), on beş gün (fifteen days). The number already says there is more than one, so -lAr would say it twice. The same goes for kaç (how many), birkaç (a few) and çok (many, a lot of).

Examples
  • İki kardeşim var.I have two brothers and sisters.
  • Kaç çocuğunuz var?How many children do you have?
  • Kaç kişisiniz? Dört kişiyiz.How many of you are there? There are four of us.
  • Burada çok kitap var.There are a lot of books here.
  • Birkaç gün İzmir'deyiz.We're in Izmir for a few days.
  • Üç öğrenci geliyor.Three students are coming.

The verb usually stays singular as well: Üç öğrenci geliyor (Three students are coming). You'll sometimes hear a plural verb with people, Üç öğrenci geliyorlar, but the singular is never wrong.

Watch out

İki kitaplar and çok insanlar are wrong. The plural appears only when no number or quantity word is there: Kitaplar masada (The books are on the table).

Tip

Before a noun, çok means many or a lot of: çok kitap. Before an adjective it means very: çok güzel, as in unit 1.

03Birinci, ikinci: ordinals

Add -(I)ncI to a number to make an ordinal (sıra sayısı): birinci (first), ikinci (second), üçüncü (third). After a vowel the first I drops: ikinci, altıncı, yedinci. The t of dört softens before the vowel: dördüncü. In a longer number only the last word takes the suffix: yirmi birinci (twenty-first).

Kaçıncı? asks which one in order: Kaçıncı kattasın? (Which floor are you on?) Üçüncü kattayım. (I'm on the third floor.) In digits, an ordinal is written with a full stop, 3. kat, or with an apostrophe and the end of the suffix, 3'üncü kat.

Examples
  • Dairemiz dördüncü katta.Our apartment is on the fourth floor.
  • İkinci kapı banyo.The second door is the bathroom.
  • Kaçıncı sınıftasın?What grade are you in?
  • Bu, onuncu dersimiz.This is our tenth lesson.
Tip

İlk (first) and son (last) are everyday alternatives: ilk gün (the first day), son ders (the last lesson). Birinci is more usual for ranks, floors and numbered lists.

04Saat kaç? Telling the time

Saat kaç? (What time is it?) is answered with saat and a number: Saat üç. (It's three o'clock.) Half past is buçuk, after the hour: Saat üç buçuk (3:30). Half past twelve has a word of its own: Saat yarım (12:30).

For minutes past the hour, the hour takes the accusative and the verb is geçiyor (is passing): Saat üçü on geçiyor (It's ten past three). For minutes to the hour, the hour takes the dative, followed by var (there are): Saat üçe on var (It's ten to three; literally, there are ten to three). A quarter is çeyrek.

At what time? Saat kaçta? asks when something happens. Whole and half hours take the locative: saat üçte (at three), üç buçukta (at half past three). With minutes, geçiyor becomes geçe and var becomes kala: üçü on geçe (at ten past three), üçe çeyrek kala (at a quarter to three).

Examples
  • Ders saat dokuzda başlıyor.The lesson starts at nine.
  • Saat kaçta geliyorsun? Yedi buçukta.What time are you coming? At half past seven.
  • Tren beşi yirmi geçe geliyor.The train arrives at twenty past five.
  • Saat ikiye beş var.It's five to two.
  • Film sekizi çeyrek geçe başlıyor.The film starts at a quarter past eight.

The 24-hour clock. Timetables, shops and the news use the 24-hour clock, written with a dot and read as plain numbers: 14.30 is on dört otuz, and saat 14.30'da means at 14:30.

Tip

In speech, sabah (morning) and akşam (evening) make the hour clear: sabah yedide (at seven in the morning), akşam sekizde (at eight in the evening).

Watch out

Past the hour takes the accusative, to the hour the dative: dördü çeyrek geçiyor (4:15), dörde çeyrek var (3:45). And Saat kaç? asks the time now, Saat kaçta? the time of an event.

Capital letters. Day and month names are common nouns, written in lowercase: pazartesi, ekimde (in October). In a specific date they take a capital, and a suffix after them takes an apostrophe: 29 Ekim 1923, 15 Ekim Perşembe, 23 Nisan'da (on 23 April).

Saying a date. Day, month, year, with ordinary numbers: 29 Ekim 1923 is read yirmi dokuz ekim bin dokuz yüz yirmi üç. To ask the date, say Bugün ayın kaçı?, literally the how-many of the month. The answer is Ayın on beşi (the fifteenth) or Bugün 15 Ekim. To ask the day: Bugün günlerden ne? (What day is it today?) Bugün perşembe.

When? A day needs no ending, Cumartesi geliyorum (I'm coming on Saturday), or is followed by günü: salı günü (on Tuesday). Months and years take the locative: ekimde (in October), 2026'da (in 2026), 23 Nisan'da (on 23 April).

Examples
  • Bugün günlerden ne? Bugün cuma.What day is it today? It's Friday.
  • Bugün ayın kaçı?What's the date today?
  • 29 Ekim'de okullar kapalı.Schools are closed on 29 October.
  • Mayısta İstanbul'a gidiyoruz.We're going to Istanbul in May.
  • Cumartesi ne yapıyorsun?What are you doing on Saturday?

Seasons. İlkbahar or bahar (spring), yaz (summer), sonbahar (autumn, fall), kış (winter). Spring and autumn take the locative, ilkbaharda, sonbaharda; summer and winter have fixed forms, yazın (in summer) and kışın (in winter).

Culture

Pazartesi is pazar ertesi, the day after Sunday, and cumartesi is cuma ertesi, the day after Friday. Pazar also means market.

Watch out

Don't capitalize a day or a month on its own: ekimde, pazartesi günü, not Ekimde or Pazartesi günü, unless it starts a sentence. The capital and the apostrophe belong to dates: 29 Ekim'de.

06Kaç yaşındasın? Kaç lira? Ages and prices

To ask someone's age: Kaç yaşındasın? (How old are you?), or politely Kaç yaşındasınız? The answer puts the number before yaşında and adds the endings of to be: Yirmi beş yaşındayım (I'm twenty-five).

Yaşında is yaş (age) + -(s)I + n + -DA, built like evinde in unit 10: literally in its year. It stays the same for every person; only the ending of to be changes.

Examples
  • Kızım beş yaşında.My daughter is five.
  • Dedem seksen yaşında.My grandfather is eighty.
  • Kaç yaşındasınız?How old are you?

Prices. Kaç lira? or Ne kadar? (How much is it?) The Turkish lira (lira, written TL or ₺) has a hundred kuruş: on beş lira (15 TL), yirmi lira elli kuruş (20.50 TL). To ask the price per kilo, Turkish uses the genitive of unit 10: Elmanın kilosu kaç lira?, literally the apple's kilo.

Examples
  • Bu ceket kaç lira?How much is this jacket?
  • Simit yirmi lira.A simit is twenty lira.
  • Elmanın kilosu kaç lira?How much is a kilo of apples?
  • İki kahve, yüz elli lira.Two coffees: a hundred and fifty lira.
Watch out

Age isn't something you have or are in Turkish, but something you are in: Yirmi yaşındayım, not Yirmi yaşım var or Yirmi yaşıyım.

Summary

  1. Numbers are built word by word, the biggest first: yirmi beş, yüz on, bin dokuz yüz yirmi üç. No bir before yüz and bin.
  2. After a number, kaç, birkaç or çok, the noun stays singular: iki kitap, kaç kişi, birkaç gün, çok insan.
  3. Ordinals add -(I)ncI: birinci, ikinci, üçüncü, dördüncü. Kaçıncı? asks which one.
  4. Saat kaç? Üçü çeyrek geçiyor (3:15), üçe çeyrek var (2:45). Saat kaçta? Üçte, üç buçukta, üçü çeyrek geçe, üçe çeyrek kala.
  5. Days and months are lowercase except in a date: pazartesi, ekimde, but 29 Ekim 1923, 23 Nisan'da.
  6. Age: Kaç yaşındasın? Yirmi yaşındayım. Prices: Kaç lira? Ne kadar?
