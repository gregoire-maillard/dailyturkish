Everyday colloquial Turkish

Bir bakar mısın?

This unit is about everyday, spoken Turkish: how to address people (abi, hocam, beyefendi), how to soften a request (Bir bakar mısın?, Gelsene), the words that fill a pause (şey, yani, falan), the suffixes that make a word small, dear, pitiful or approximate (küçücük, adamcağız, yeşilimsi), and how one request moves from formal to colloquial.

01Abi, abla, hocam: addressing people

English often addresses a stranger with nothing at all: Excuse me, is this the bus to the centre? Turkish likes to name the person, and the word it picks says how old they are compared with you, what they do and how formal the moment is. Many of these words come from the family: you call a shopkeeper a little older than you abi (big brother) and a woman your grandmother's age teyze (auntie), without being related at all.

WordSay it toExample
abia man a little older than you; friendlyAbi, bu otobüs Kadıköy'e gidiyor mu? (Excuse me, does this bus go to Kadıköy?)
ablaa woman a little older than you; friendlyAbla, simit kaç lira? (How much is a simit?)
kardeşima younger person, usually a young manKardeşim, bir yardım eder misin? (Mate, could you give me a hand?)
teyzean older woman, about your mother's or grandmother's ageTeyze, buyurun, oturun. (Here, have a seat.)
amcaan older manAmca, çantanızı taşıyayım mı? (Shall I carry your bag?)
hocama teacher or lecturer; in many offices, a colleagueHocam, bir sorum var. (Excuse me, I have a question.)
ustaa craftsman, a mechanic, a cook, a driverUsta, müsait bir yerde inebilir miyim? (Could you let me off somewhere convenient?)
beyefendia man you don't know, politelyBeyefendi, cüzdanınız düştü. (Sir, you've dropped your wallet.)
hanımefendia woman you don't know, politelyHanımefendi, sıra sizde. (Madam, it's your turn.)

Sen or siz? The family words are warm and usually go with sen: Abi, bakar mısın? With an older stranger many people still use siz (Teyze, buyurun, oturun), and beyefendi and hanımefendi always take siz: Beyefendi, buyurun sounds right, Beyefendi, gel buraya sounds rude.

With a name. For someone you know, put abi, abla, teyze or amca after the first name: Ali abi, Fatma teyze. In writing these keep a small letter, because they are family words; the titles Bey and Hanım of A1 unit 3 take a capital: Murat Bey, Ayşe Hanım. Bey and Hanım are the polite choice with colleagues, neighbours and customers; Bay and Bayan belong on forms and doors, not in speech.

Hocam is hoca (teacher) with the possessive my. Students say it to teachers at every level, and in universities and many offices it has become a friendly, respectful way to address a colleague. Usta (master) does the same for anyone who works with their hands: the mechanic, the cook at the kebab shop, the driver of a dolmuş.

Terms of endearment. Family and friends use the possessive too: canım (my dear, from can, life), kızım (my girl), oğlum (my boy), evladım (my child). Older people say them to any young person, warmly. A diminutive makes them tenderer still: anneciğim, babacığım (see below).

Examples
  • Hocam, bir sorum var.Excuse me (to a teacher), I have a question.
  • Usta, müsait bir yerde inebilir miyim?Could you let me off somewhere convenient? (to a driver)
  • Pardon beyefendi, bu yer boş mu?Excuse me, sir, is this seat free?
  • Canım, akşam ne yiyelim?Darling, what shall we eat tonight?
  • Kardeşim, otobüs durağı nerede?Mate, where's the bus stop?
Watch out

Abi and abla are friendly, not rude, but they assume a relaxed setting: in a bank or a hotel, say beyefendi, hanımefendi or nothing. Kardeşim said sharply can sound like listen, pal: Kardeşim, sıraya gir! (Get in the queue, pal!)

02Bir bakar mısın? Softening a request

A bare imperative (Kapıyı kapat) is fine with family, but most requests are softened, and people combine several tools at once.

ToolExampleMeaning and feel
an aorist question (A2 unit 4)Kapıyı kapatır mısın?Could you close the door? Everyday, neutral.
bir before the verbBir bakar mısın?Could you just take a look? Lighter: just, for a moment.
-(y)Abil + question (A2 unit 5)Kapıyı kapatabilir misiniz?Could you close the door? Polite.
-sAnA, -sAnIzAKapıyı kapatsana!Close the door, will you? Friendly, insistent.
zahmet olmazsaZahmet olmazsa kapıyı kapatır mısınız?If it's no trouble, could you close the door? Very polite.
rica etsemBir bardak su rica etsem?Could I have a glass of water? Polite, a little formal.
mümkünse, müsaitsenizMüsaitseniz biraz konuşabilir miyiz?If you're free, could we talk a little? Polite.

Bir here isn't one. Unstressed and right before the verb, it means just, for a moment, and makes the request smaller: Bir bakar mısınız? is the standard way to call a waiter or a shop assistant (you met Bakar mısınız? in A2 unit 4); Bir dakika gelir misin? (Could you come here a minute?). In quick speech it often sounds like bi.

-sAnA. Added to the verb stem, -sAnA (-sAnIzA for siz) makes a friendly push, half suggestion, half order: Gelsene! (Come on, come over!), Otursana (Sit down, why don't you), Baksana! (Hey, look!). The voice decides whether it is warm or impatient: Dinlesene! can be Listen to this! or Will you listen! The negative, -mAsAnA, tells someone to stop: Bağırmasana! (Stop shouting, will you!). There is no question form.

Conditionals that ask. A conditional without its main clause sounds tentative, like what if I…?: Pencereyi açsam? (What if I opened the window?), Bir çay daha içsek? (How about another tea?), from B1 unit 9. Rica etsem (literally if I requested) turns this into a polite request: Bir bardak su rica etsem? (Could I have a glass of water?), Sizden bir şey rica etsem? (Could I ask you a favour?).

Zahmet olmazsa (if it's no trouble) and mümkünse (if possible) go in front of a polite question: Zahmet olmazsa tuzu uzatır mısınız? (Would you mind passing the salt?). Before asking, people often apologize for the bother: Affedersiniz… (Excuse me…), Kusura bakmayın, rahatsız ediyorum ama… (Sorry to bother you, but…). The usual answers are Tabii, Olur and, to thanks for the trouble, Ne zahmeti! (No trouble at all!).

Examples
  • Bir bakar mısınız?Excuse me! (to a waiter)
  • Gelsene, film başlıyor!Come on, the film's starting!
  • Zahmet olmazsa tuzu uzatır mısınız?Would you mind passing the salt?
  • Bağırmasana, bebek uyuyor!Stop shouting, the baby's asleep!
  • Sizden bir şey rica etsem?Could I ask you something?
Watch out

-sAnA is for people you say sen to, or a group of friends (-sAnIzA). With a stranger or a superior it is too pushy: to a teacher, say Hocam, bir bakar mısınız?, not Hocam, baksanıza.

03Şey, yani, falan: filling the gaps

Conversation needs words that buy time, soften a point or round off a list. A learner who never uses them sounds like a book; one who uses nothing else sounds like a teenager. These seven are the core.

WordAs a fillerExample
şeyum, er; thingy (a word you can't find)Şey… adı neydi, o dizi? (Um… what was it called, that series?)
yaniI mean; so; that isYani gelmiyor musun? (So you're not coming?)
işteyou know; well, there you goİşte, öyle bir şey. (Well, it's something like that.)
haniyou know (the one)Hani şu yeni dizi var ya? (You know that new series?)
yaoh; really (adds feeling)Çok güzeldi ya! (It was really lovely!)
aslındaactually (softens a correction)Aslında ben pek sevmedim. (Actually, I didn't like it much.)
falanand stuff; about, or soYarım saat falan bekledik. (We waited half an hour or so.)

Şey (thing) stands in for a word you can't find, and on its own it fills a pause: Şey… nasıl desem… (Um… how can I put it…). It even takes the case ending of the noun it replaces: Şeyi gördün mü, kumandayı? (Have you seen the thingy, the remote?).

Yani (that is) explains, sums up or draws a conclusion: Film uzundu, yani üç saat! (The film was long, I mean three hours!). On its own, as a question, Yani? means So? or So what?

İşte and hani you met as particles in B2 unit 8. As fillers they keep a story moving: Dün işte arkadaşlarla çıktık, işte bir kafeye gittik… (Yesterday, you know, we went out with friends, went to a café…). Hani points to something the listener should remember: Hani dün bahsettiğim film… (You know, the film I mentioned yesterday…).

Ya at the end of a sentence adds feeling, and is often stretched in speech: Çok güzel ya! (It's so lovely!), Of ya, yine geç kaldık! (Ugh, we're late again!). At the start, it brushes something aside: Ya, bırak şimdi! (Oh, forget it!). This is not the reminding ya of B2 unit 8, which follows something the listener already knows.

Falan (also filan) ends a list or makes a number approximate: Çay, kahve falan içtik (We had tea, coffee and so on), Yirmi kişi falan vardı (There were twenty people or so). Falan filan is and so on and so forth.

A few more. Peki (OK, well then) accepts or moves on; neyse (anyway) closes a topic; aynen (exactly) agrees; cidden (seriously) asks for or gives emphasis: Cidden mi? (Seriously?); mesela (for example) brings in an example.

Examples
  • Şey… bir sorum olacaktı.Um… I had a question.
  • Yani bu akşam gelmiyorsun, öyle mi?So you're not coming tonight, is that it?
  • Hani şu yeni dizi var ya, çok güzelmiş.You know that new series? Apparently it's really good.
  • Aslında ben o filmi pek sevmedim.Actually, I didn't really like that film.
  • Neyse, yarın konuşuruz.Anyway, we'll talk tomorrow.
Tip

In writing and in formal speech, leave the fillers out. And don't stack them: Yani şey işte falan… tells your listener you have nothing to say.

04Küçücük, adamcağız, yeşilimsi: small, dear, pitiful, -ish

Four suffixes shade a word rather than change it: -CIk makes it small or dear, -CAğIz pitiful, and -(I)msI and -(I)mtırak only roughly so, like English -ish. All four belong to everyday, emotional speech.

-CIk on adjectives makes a small thing smaller or a pleasant quality cosier: küçücük (tiny), sıcacık bir çorba (a lovely warm soup). The common forms are best learned as words: a final k drops (küçük → küçücük), and a one-syllable adjective often adds a vowel (az → azıcık). The C becomes ç after a voiceless consonant, as in -CI (A2 unit 12), but most of these words end in a vowel or a voiced consonant.

AdjectiveWith -CIkMeaning
küçükküçücüktiny, teeny
ufakufacıktiny, little
sıcaksıcacıknice and warm, cosy
yumuşakyumuşacıknice and soft
azazıcıkjust a little
inceincecikvery thin, slender
kısakısacıkvery short
dardaracıkvery narrow, cramped

-CIk with words for people. On anne, baba and other words you call people by, -CIk with the possessive is pure affection: anneciğim (mum dear), babacığım, teyzeciğim, ablacığım. The k softens to ğ before the vowel of the possessive: anne-cik → anne-ciğ-im.

-CAğIz (pitiful). After a noun for a person, it shows sympathy: adamcağız (the poor man), kadıncağız (the poor woman), kızcağız (the poor girl), çocukcağız (the poor child), hayvancağız (the poor animal). It belongs to storytelling, usually in the third person: Adamcağız saatlerce bekledi (The poor man waited for hours). The adjective zavallı (poor, pitiful) does the same job in front of the noun, and the two often go together: zavallı adamcağız.

-(I)msI and -(I)mtırak (-ish) work on colours, tastes and a few other adjectives: yeşilimsi (greenish), mavimsi (bluish), ekşimsi (sourish), tatlımsı (sweetish). -(I)msI follows four-way harmony and adds its first vowel only after a consonant: mavi-msi, sarı-msı, yeşil-imsi, mor-umsu. -(I)mtırak means the same, works on fewer words, and keeps its own vowels after the first: ekşimtırak, yeşilimtırak, sarımtırak.

Word-(I)msI-(I)mtırak
yeşilyeşilimsiyeşilimtırak
sarısarımsısarımtırak
ekşiekşimsiekşimtırak
acıacımsıacımtırak
mavimavimsimavimtırak
Examples
  • Küçücük bir dairede oturuyorlar.They live in a tiny flat.
  • Anneciğim, bugün çok yorgunum.Mum dear, I'm really tired today.
  • Adamcağız otobüsü saatlerce bekledi.The poor man waited hours for the bus.
  • Denizin yeşilimsi bir rengi vardı.The sea was a greenish colour.
  • Bu elmalar biraz ekşimtırak.These apples are a bit sourish.
  • Azıcık daha çay alır mısın?Would you like a drop more tea?
Watch out

-CIk and -CAğIz are colloquial and emotional: right in a story or a chat, out of place in a report. And not every word takes them; if you haven't heard a form, say çok küçük, biraz yeşil or zavallı adam instead.

Compare

Yeşilimsi and yeşilimtırak are French verdâtre; sıcacık and küçücük are close to tout chaud and tout petit.

05One request, three registers

The same request comes out very differently in an official notice, with a colleague and among friends. Choosing a register means choosing several things together: siz or sen, the verb form, the way you address the person, and the small words.

The same requests in three registers
RequestFormalNeutralColloquial
Close the door.Lütfen kapıyı kapatınız.Kapıyı kapatabilir misiniz?Kapıyı bir kapatsana.
Wait a moment.Lütfen bir dakika bekleyiniz.Bir dakika bekler misiniz?Bir dakika dur ya!
Speak more slowly.Daha yavaş konuşmanızı rica ederim.Biraz daha yavaş konuşabilir misiniz?Biraz yavaş konuşsana!
Help me.Yardımcı olmanızı rica ederim.Bana yardım eder misiniz?Bana bir yardım etsene!

Formal Turkish, in letters, notices and announcements (B2 unit 5), uses siz, the long imperative -(y)InIz, rica ederim after a -mA clause (B2 unit 4), and words like yardımcı olmak (to be of help) or bilgi vermek (to inform). Neutral Turkish, the safe choice with anyone you don't know well, asks a question with siz: -(y)AbilIr misiniz? or the aorist, with lütfen or bir. Colloquial Turkish, with friends and family, uses sen, -sAnA, bir, the fillers, and an address word: abi, canım.

WhatFormalNeutralColloquial
yousizsiz (sen with friends)sen

verb-mAnızı rica ederim, -(y)InIz-(y)AbilIr misiniz?, -(I)r mIsInIz?-sAnA, the bare imperative
softenerslütfen, mümkünsebir, zahmet olmazsaya, biraz

Examples
  • Sayın izleyicilerimiz, yayınımıza kısa bir ara veriyoruz.Dear viewers, we are taking a short break. (formal: a TV announcer)
  • Televizyonun sesini biraz kısabilir misiniz?Could you turn the TV down a little? (neutral)
  • Şu televizyonun sesini kıssana biraz!Turn that TV down a bit, will you! (colloquial)
  • Toplantıyı yarına mı atsak?Shall we push the meeting to tomorrow? (colloquial)
Tip

When in doubt, choose neutral: siz and a question with -(y)AbilIr misiniz? never offends. Moving to sen is usually the older or more senior person's offer: Bana sen diyebilirsin (You can say sen to me).

Summary

  1. Strangers are addressed by age and role: abi, abla, kardeşim, teyze, amca (friendly, mostly with sen), hocam, usta, and politely beyefendi and hanımefendi (always with siz). After a name: Ali abi (small letter), Ayşe Hanım (capital).
  2. Soften a request with an aorist question and bir (Bir bakar mısınız?), with -(y)AbilIr misiniz?, zahmet olmazsa or rica etsem. -sAnA (Gelsene!) is a friendly push for sen; -mAsAnA (Bağırmasana!) asks someone to stop.
  3. Fillers: şey (um; thingy), yani (I mean, so), işte and hani (you know), ya (feeling), aslında (actually), falan (and stuff, or so); also peki, neyse, aynen, cidden, mesela.
  4. -CIk makes small or cosy (küçücük, sıcacık, azıcık) and dear (anneciğim); -CAğIz pitiful (adamcağız); -(I)msI and -(I)mtırak mean -ish (yeşilimsi, ekşimtırak).
  5. Formal, neutral and colloquial requests differ in person (siz, sen), verb form (-mAnızı rica ederim, -(y)AbilIr misiniz?, -sAnA), address and particles. Neutral is the safe default.
