Word order and focus

Gördüm onu!

Turkish word order is free, but not random. In a neutral sentence the verb comes last; the word right before it is the focus, the new or important part; what follows the verb is already known. Speakers move words in and out of these slots to do what English does with stress or with it was … who. This unit also covers topics, echo words such as kitap mitap, and the small words that add emphasis.

01The focus slot before the verb

A neutral Turkish sentence ends with its verb: subject, then time and place, then object, then verb (A1 unit 8). The position right before the verb is special: it holds the focus, the part of the sentence that is new or contrasted, and it carries the main stress. Change what you put there, and you change the question the sentence answers.

SentenceIt answersMeaning
Ali dün bir kitap aldı.Ali dün ne aldı?Yesterday Ali bought a book.
Ali kitabı dün aldı.Ali kitabı ne zaman aldı?Ali bought the book yesterday.
Kitabı dün Ali aldı.Kitabı dün kim aldı?It was Ali who bought the book yesterday.
Ali kitabı dün kitapçıdan aldı.Ali kitabı dün nereden aldı?Ali bought the book at the bookshop yesterday.

Questions and answers. A question word sits in the focus slot (Kitabı kim aldı?), and the answer puts its new word in the same place (Kitabı Ali aldı). In speech you will often hear only that word: Ali. The particle mI marks the same slot in a yes-no question (A1 unit 4): Kitabı Ali mi aldı?, was it Ali who bought the book?

The bare object goes with its verb. An indefinite object with no case ending is glued to its verb, and the two count as one unit, so the focus slot moves one step to the left: Dün akşam kim kitap okudu? — Ali kitap okudu. Without a contrast, the pair itself is the news: Ali dün akşam kitap okudu (Ali spent last evening reading).

Contrast and değil. To deny one element and give another, put it in the focus slot of a negative verb, or in front of değil: Kitabı Ali almadı, Ayşe aldı (it wasn't Ali who bought the book, it was Ayşe), Ben değil, Ayşe aldı (not me, Ayşe bought it).

Clefts. For heavier emphasis, Turkish turns the rest of the sentence into a participle (B1 unit 5) and puts the focus in the predicate: Kitabı alan Ali'ydi (the one who bought the book was Ali), Beni en çok şaşırtan, fiyatı oldu (what surprised me most was the price), Asıl önemli olan, türkünün yaşaması (what really matters is that the folk song lives on).

Dialogue
Selin

Bu pastayı kim yaptı?

Who made this cake?

Can

Pastayı annem yaptı.

My mother made the cake.

Selin

Annen mi? Ben de seni sanmıştım!

Your mother? And there I was thinking it was you!

Can

Ben değil, annem. Ben sadece yedim.

Not me, my mother. I just ate it.

Examples
  • Kitabı dün Ali aldı.It was Ali who bought the book yesterday.
  • Kitabı Ali mi aldı?Was it Ali who bought the book?
  • Toplantıya ben değil, Selin gidecek.It's Selin who'll go to the meeting, not me.
  • Bu şehre ilk kez geçen yıl geldim.I first came to this city last year.The focus is geçen yıl: the sentence answers when.
  • Beni en çok şaşırtan, fiyatı oldu.What surprised me most was the price.
Tip

To find the focus of a sentence you read, look at the word just before the verb, then ask which question the sentence answers.

Compare

English moves the stress (ALI bought it) or uses a cleft (it was Ali who…), as French uses c'est … qui. Turkish usually just moves the word.

02After the verb: inverted sentences

In speech, words often come after the verb: Geldi mi Ayşe? (has Ayşe come?), Gördüm onu! (I saw him!), Çok güzeldi film (the film was really good). Grammar books call this an inverted sentence (devrik cümle). It is not an error: the words after the verb are background, already known or added as an afterthought, while the verb and what comes before it carry the news.

What can go there. Subjects, definite objects, adverbs and whole phrases can follow the verb: Aradın mı babanı?, Gidiyorum ben, Gelirim yarın. What cannot: the focus, so never a question word (Kimi gördün?, not Gördün kimi?), and a bare object, which stays glued to its verb (Kitap okudum, not Okudum kitap; but Okudum kitabı is fine).

NeutralInvertedWhat the inversion does
Ayşe geldi mi?Geldi mi Ayşe?asks about the arrival; Ayşe is already the topic
Onu gördüm!Gördüm onu!puts the news first, with feeling
Film çok güzeldi.Çok güzeldi film.a reaction; everyone knows which film
Ben gidiyorum.Gidiyorum ben.casual: I'm off
Yarın gelirim.Gelirim yarın.the time added as an afterthought

Where you meet it. Inversion is everywhere in conversation; in songs and folk poetry, where it serves rhythm and rhyme; and in literary prose, for effect. In formal writing, such as reports, articles and letters, keep the verb at the end.

Examples
  • Gördün mü yeni komşuyu?Have you seen the new neighbour?
  • Bitti mi ödevin?Is your homework finished?
  • Çok yoruldum bugün.I'm so tired today.
  • Ben söylemiştim sana!I told you so!
  • Nereye gidiyorsun bu saatte?Where are you going at this hour?The question word stays before the verb; bu saatte is background.
Watch out

Keep the verb last in formal writing, and never put a question word after the verb: Kimi gördün?, not Gördün kimi? In everyday speech a bare object stays before its verb too: Kitap okudum, not Okudum kitap.

Culture

Folk verse bends even these rules for the rhyme. In an old Istanbul mani of this unit's interlude, Nerden içsin kuzu su, both the subject and the bare object follow the verb; everyday Turkish would say Kuzu nereden su içsin?

03Topics: ise, -(y)sA and gelince

The start of a sentence usually holds the topic, what the sentence is about: it is already known, and the comment follows. A definite noun at the start is usually the topic: in Bu filmi çok sevdim, the film is what we are talking about, and çok sevdim is what we say about it. Turkish also has markers for a topic, especially one that contrasts with another or opens a new subject.

MarkerUseExample
ise, -(y)sAa topic set against anotherBen çay içtim, Ayşe ise kahve. / Ayşe'yse kahve içti.
-(y)A gelinceturning to a new topic: as forBana gelince, ben bu işe karışmam.
-DAn yanaas far as … goesParadan yana sorunumuz yok.
konusundaon the matter ofFiyat konusunda anlaşamadık.

İse and -(y)sA. You met ise as whereas in B2 unit 3. The same particle marks a contrasted topic: it comes right after the topic, before the comment. Attached as -(y)sA it sounds more everyday: Ali İzmir'e gitti, ben ise Ankara'ya or bense Ankara'ya. After a case ending: Bana ise kimse sormadı or Banaysa kimse sormadı (as for me, nobody asked).

-(y)A gelince. Gelince (when it comes, B1 unit 7) after a dative turns the conversation: Bana gelince… (as for me), Paraya gelince, o başka bir konu (as for the money, that's another matter). It often announces a contrast with what was just said.

-DAn yana frames a topic as an area: Paradan yana sorunumuz yok, zamandan yana var (money's not a problem for us; time is). Topic and focus together. A typical sentence runs from topic to focus: in Kardeşimse dün akşam bize geldi, kardeşimse is the contrasted topic and bize, right before the verb, the new information.

Examples
  • Ben çayı şekersiz içerim, eşim ise üç şekerli.I take my tea without sugar, whereas my wife takes it with three.
  • Herkes erken gitti; Mehmet'se sabaha kadar kaldı.Everyone left early; Mehmet, though, stayed until morning.
  • Bana gelince, ben bu işe karışmam.As for me, I'm staying out of this.
  • Paradan yana sorunumuz yok, zamandan yana var.Money's not a problem for us; time is.
  • Yemeklere gelince, hepsi buz gibiydi.As for the food, all of it was stone-cold.
Watch out

İse and -(y)sA follow the topic word; they never open the clause: Ayşe ise kahve içti, not ise Ayşe kahve içti. And don't mistake the topic -(y)sA for a condition: here Mehmet'se means as for Mehmet, not if it's Mehmet; the context decides.

04Kitap mitap, ıvır zıvır: echo words

Turkish loves pairs of words. One kind anyone can make: repeat a word with its first consonant replaced by m, or with m added before a first vowel, and you get … and the like, … and all that, often with a shrug: kitap mitap (books and that sort of thing), çay may (tea or whatever), okul mokul (school and all that). TDK writes the pair as two words, the second one without a meaning of its own.

m-doubling
kitap   →  kitap mitap
çay     →  çay may
ders    →  ders mers
okul    →  okul mokul
ekmek   →  ekmek mekmek

What it means. The pair stands for the thing and everything like it, and the tone is casual, often dismissive: Kitap mitap istemiyorum, biraz dinlenmek istiyorum (I don't want books or anything, I want a rest). Okul mokul derken yıl bitti (what with school and all that, the year was over). A word that already begins with m can't be doubled this way; use falan instead (B2 unit 9): masa falan, müzik falan.

Fixed pairs (ikilemeler). Besides the m-pairs anyone can make, Turkish has hundreds of fixed pairs: echoes whose second word means nothing on its own (ıvır zıvır, eğri büğrü, abur cubur), near-synonyms (eş dost, konu komşu, çoluk çocuk), opposites (az çok, er geç, aşağı yukarı) and sound words (şırıl şırıl, mışıl mışıl). TDK writes most of them as two words; a few are one (darmadağın, çerçöp, öteberi).

PairMeaningExample
ıvır zıvırodds and ends; trivialÇantamda bir sürü ıvır zıvır var.
abur cuburjunk food, snacksYemekten önce abur cubur yeme.
eğri büğrücrooked, unevenEğri büğrü sokaklarda kaybolduk.
eş dostfriends and acquaintancesDüğüne bütün eş dost geldi.
konu komşuthe neighbours, the whole neighbourhoodBu haberi bütün konu komşu duydu.
çoluk çocukthe whole family, children and allBayramda çoluk çocuk köye gittik.
er geçsooner or laterEr geç gerçeği öğrenecek.
aşağı yukarıroughly, more or lessAşağı yukarı iki saat bekledik.
mışıl mışılsoundly (of sleep)Bebek mışıl mışıl uyuyor.
Examples
  • Kitap mitap istemiyorum, biraz dinlenmek istiyorum.I don't want books or anything, I want a bit of a rest.
  • Çay may içtik, biraz sohbet ettik.We had tea and so on and chatted for a while.
  • Okul mokul derken yıl bitti.What with school and all that, the year was over.
  • Ders mers bırak şimdi, biraz dinlen!Forget lessons and all that for now, have a rest!
  • Er geç gerçeği öğrenecek.Sooner or later she'll find out the truth.
Watch out

M-doubling is casual and can sound dismissive. Leave it out of formal writing, and be careful with things the listener cares about: Düğün müğün umurumda değil tells the bride you don't care about her wedding.

Compare

English has a few echo pairs (helter-skelter, higgledy-piggledy) and the Yiddish-flavoured fancy-shmancy, which works just like m-doubling.

05Ta, hem de, de mi, işte: adding emphasis

Speech adds weight with small words as much as with stress. You know dA, hem de and işte from B2 unit 8; here they work as emphasis, with one newcomer, ta.

WordWhat it stressesExample
tahow far, or how long ago: all the way, as far back asTa İstanbul'dan geldi. (He came all the way from Istanbul.)
ta kendisithe very oneBu, aradığım kitabın ta kendisi! (This is the very book I was looking for!)
hem deand what's more; and at thatKazandık, hem de beş sıfır! (We won, and five-nil at that!)
dA mIsurprise or reproach: too, even, againBugün de mi geç kaldın? (You're late again today?)
işteexactly that, the very oneİşte bu yüzden gelmedim. (That's exactly why I didn't come.)

Ta (pronounced with a long a) stretches a distance or a time, and goes with -DAn, -(y)A kadar or beri: ta Ankara'dan (all the way from Ankara), ta sabaha kadar (right through till morning), ta çocukluğumdan beri (ever since I was a child). Ta kendisi is the very one; ta ki brings in a final limit, until at last: Bekledi de bekledi, ta ki kapı açılana kadar (she waited and waited, until at last the door opened). It is written apart and has nothing to do with the locative: ta İstanbul'dan, but İstanbul'da.

Hem de adds a stronger point after a statement: Geldi, hem de erkenden (she came, and early at that). On its own, Hem de nasıl! is and how!

dA mI asks, in surprise or reproach, whether something applies too, even or again: Sen de mi? (you too?), Bunu da mı bilmiyorsun? (you don't even know this?), Bugün de mi geç kaldın? (late again today?). The particle stands after the word it highlights, and mI follows dA in harmony with it: da mı, de mi.

İşte points at something as the very thing: İşte bu! (that's it!), İşte o zaman anladım (it was then that I understood). Before bu yüzden, böyle or o zaman it works like an English cleft.

Examples
  • Ta Kars'tan düğüne geldiler.They came all the way from Kars for the wedding.
  • Onu ta çocukluğumdan beri tanırım.I've known him since I was a small child.
  • Bütün sınavları geçti, hem de hepsini yüksek notla.She passed all her exams, and every one with high marks at that.
  • Bu sene de mi tatile çıkmıyorsunuz?Aren't you going on holiday this year either?
  • İşte o zaman her şeyi anladım.It was then that I understood everything.
  • Bu şarkı, çocukken dinlediğim türkünün ta kendisi.This song is the very folk song I listened to as a child.
Tip

In writing, stress can't be seen: word order and these particles show where the weight of a sentence falls.

Summary

  1. The verb comes last; the word right before it is the focus, the new or contrasted part: Kitabı Ali aldı answers who.
  2. Question words and the words that answer them sit in the focus slot; mI marks it in a yes-no question: Kitabı Ali mi aldı?
  3. Words after the verb are background or afterthought: Geldi mi Ayşe? Never put a question word there, and keep a bare object before its verb.
  4. Inverted sentences (devrik cümle) belong to speech, songs and poetry; formal writing keeps the verb last.
  5. Topics come first; ise, -(y)sA and -(y)A gelince mark a contrasted or new topic: Bana gelince…, Mehmet'se…
  6. M-doubling (kitap mitap) means … and the like, casually; fixed pairs such as ıvır zıvır, eş dost and er geç are learned whole.
  7. Ta, hem de, dA mI and işte add emphasis: ta İstanbul'dan, hem de nasıl, Sen de mi?, İşte bu!
