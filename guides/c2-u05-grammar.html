Wordplay and humour

Söz oyunları

Turkish humour plays with sound and with grammar. Homonyms let one word mean two things; folk poets build rhymes on them (cinas) and children trip over tongue twisters; the fıkra sets up a little story and turns it round in one line; and in conversation, irony and understatement say one thing and mean another. This unit shows how each of them works, so that you get the joke, and can sometimes make one.

01One sound, two words: homonyms

A homonym (sesteş, also eş sesli) is a word that is said and spelled like another but has an unrelated meaning and origin: yüz (a hundred) and yüz (face). TDK defines sesteş exactly so: the same pronunciation, different meanings and roots. A word with several related senses is something else, çok anlamlı: göz is an eye and also the eye of a needle, one word stretched by a figure of speech. Puns (söz oyunu, kelime oyunu) feed on both.

The imperative makes homonyms. The second-person imperative is the bare verb stem (A1 unit 12), so many short nouns have a verb twin. Say Yüz! by a pool and it means Swim!; say it looking at a photo and it is a face.

Suffixes settle it, or keep the joke going. Usually a suffix tells the twins apart: gülü is the rose as an object, gülüyor is laughing; atı is the horse, atıyor is throwing. But some suffixed forms are ambiguous in their own right, and that is where puns live. Yazın can be the adverb in summer (A1 unit 11), yaz + -(I)n (your summer), or yaz- + -(y)In (Write!, said to several people or politely). Atın is your horse, the horse's, or Throw!

Examples
  • Yazın bol bol okuyun, bol bol yazın.In summer, read a lot and write a lot.The first yazın is the season, the second the polite imperative of yazmak.
  • Bahçedeki gülleri görünce güldü.When she saw the roses in the garden, she laughed.
  • Çayın kenarında oturup çay içtik.We sat on the bank of the stream and drank tea.
  • Dolu yağınca bütün kafeler doluydu.When it hailed, every café was full.
  • Çocuk atın önüne bir elma attı.The child threw an apple in front of the horse.

Riddles (bilmece), advertisements and headlines use the same pairs. A summer campaign might promise Bu yaz bol bol yazın; a teahouse by a river might call itself Çay Kenarı and mean both. When you meet a sentence that seems slightly odd, try the other twin.

Watch out

In writing, the circumflex keeps apart some pairs that sound alike or nearly alike: kar (snow) and kâr (profit), hala (aunt) and hâlâ (still), adet (number, piece) and âdet (custom). Print often drops the mark, so a pun on these works better aloud than on paper.

Tip

To catch a pun, give each word the other twin's grammar: read the bare stem as an order, or the verb as a noun with a case ending. If both readings make sense, you have found the joke.

02Cinas and tekerleme: playing with sound

Cinas is the traditional name for the figure built on homonyms. TDK defines it as using a word with several meanings in nearby places, giving it a different meaning each time. Folk poets put it in the rhyme. In the kesik mani (cut mani), the first line is short, three or four syllables, and its words come back at the end of lines 2 and 4 with a new meaning each time (C1 unit 6 interlude).

The two bağlar of the mani
LineRead asMeaning
2: Yâr başın böyle bağlarbağlarshe ties (the aorist of bağlamak)
4: Yıkılsın böyle bağlarbağlarvineyards, gardens

The short first line holds both readings until the listener hears the rest; the joy is in the second reading arriving. Başın is an older, poetic form of başını, common in folk verse. Line 3 is free, as in every mani.

In classical poetry, cinas was one of the figures a poet was expected to handle, beside allusion and wit; TDK's own example sentence lists cinas, telmih, nükte together. The tuyuğ, a four-line form of classical poetry rhymed aaxa, most often put cinas in its rhyme, and its very name meant speaking in a veiled or punning way (C1 unit 4 interlude introduces the court poets).

Tekerleme has several senses in TDK. In tales it is the rhymed opening or link, Bir varmış, bir yokmuş (A2 unit 2 interlude); in children's games it is a counting-out rhyme; and in everyday speech it is also a tongue twister. For the tongue twister TDK has a word of its own, yanıltmaç: words arranged so that the speaker slips and says something else.

Tongue twisters pile up sounds that sit close together in the mouth: s and ş, r and l, k and t. They are traditional and anonymous, and children race to say them fast. Their grammar is ordinary, which makes them good practice.

Examples
  • Şu köşe yaz köşesi, şu köşe kış köşesi, ortada su şişesi.This corner is the summer corner, that corner the winter corner, and in the middle a water bottle.s against ş, in three compounds with -(s)I.
  • Dal sarkar kartal kalkar, kartal kalkar dal sarkar.The branch droops and the eagle takes off; the eagle takes off and the branch droops.r, l and k in four aorists.
  • Bir berber bir berbere: “Bre berber, beri gel, beraber bir berber dükkânı açalım.” demiş.A barber said to a barber: 'Hey, barber, come over here, let's open a barber's shop together.'Bre is an old 'hey!'; the little story ends in -mIş, like a tale.
  • Şu evi badanalamalı mı, yoksa badanalamamalı mı?Should we whitewash this house, or should we not?-mAlI and its negative side by side (A2 unit 6).
Tip

Saying a tongue twister slowly, then faster, is good pronunciation practice: it trains the short Turkish vowels and the pairs s/ş and c/ç that learners blur.

Watch out

A tale formula and a tongue twister are both tekerleme in everyday speech. When you need the precise word for the tongue twister, say yanıltmaç.

03The fıkra: setup, turn and punchline

A fıkra is a joke told as a story. TDK defines it as a short, concise, witty and funny little story. The same word also means a newspaper column and a paragraph of a law, so context decides. The best-known fıkras are Nasreddin Hoca's (A1 unit 4 interlude); others are about types (the miser, the know-all), trades and regional characters.

Three parts. A fıkra opens with a short setup that names the people and the place; then something odd happens, the turn; and it ends on a punchline, usually one line of dialogue, that makes the odd thing logical in its own crooked way. The wit of it is its nükte or espri. Here is the cauldron story, which you met in English in A1, as it is told in Turkish.

Kazan doğurdu, in three parts
PartThe storyWhat to notice

TurnKazanı geri götürürken içine küçük bir tencere koymuş: “Kazanınız doğurdu.” demiş. Bir süre sonra kazanı yine almış, ama bu kez geri getirmemiş: “Kazanınız öldü.”two short lines of direct speech; the second mirrors the first
PunchlineKomşu: “Kazan ölür mü?” Hoca: “Doğurduğuna inandın da öldüğüne neden inanmıyorsun?”two noun clauses in the dative of inanmak (B2 unit 4)

The tense of a fıkra. Traditional fıkras are told in -mIş, like tales: the teller didn't see it happen (C2 unit 2). Modern jokes are often told in the present, to make them immediate, and open with Adamın biri… (a certain man): Adamın biri doktora gidiyor. Either way, the punchline is direct speech, often followed by demiş or diyor.

The Hoca's logic. Many Nasreddin fıkras accept an absurd premise and follow it to the end. Ignored at a feast in his everyday clothes, the Hoca comes back in a fur coat and dips his sleeve in the soup: if the coat is the honoured guest, the coat should eat, Ye kürküm ye! In another, he is seen pouring yogurt starter (maya) into a lake; asked why, he answers Ya tutarsa! (But what if it takes!). Both lines are now idioms: Ye kürküm ye for people judged by their clothes, and göle maya çalmak (to put starter in the lake) for a hopeless attempt, often with Ya tutarsa! as its comment.

Examples
  • Adamın biri bakkala girmiş.A man walked into a corner shop.Adamın biri, literally 'one of the man', opens countless jokes.
  • Kazanınız doğurdu.Your cauldron has given birth.
  • Doğurduğuna inandın da öldüğüne neden inanmıyorsun?You believed it gave birth, so why won't you believe it died?da here means and yet (B2 unit 8).
  • Hoca göle maya çalıyormuş.The Hoca, so the story goes, was putting yogurt starter into the lake.maya çalmak: to stir starter into milk to make yogurt.
  • Ya tutarsa!But what if it takes!ya + -sA for what if (B1 unit 9).
Culture

Temel and Dursun

The other great family of fıkras features Temel, Dursun and Fadime from the eastern Black Sea coast. Many are told by people from the region themselves, with affection, and many play on its dialect; others feel that they turn a whole region into a type. If you tell one, tell it without imitating the accent, and think about who is listening (C2 unit 4).

Watch out

Don't explain a fıkra after telling it: the listener should do the last step. If you missed one yourself, ask Espriyi anlamadım, açıklar mısın? A joke that falls flat is a soğuk espri, literally a cold joke, a compound TDK records.

04Irony, understatement and teasing

Irony (ironi) says the reverse of what it means: TDK defines it as mocking a person or an event by meaning the opposite of what is said. Turkish conversation uses it constantly, carried by a flat or over-sweet tone, by the situation, and by a few phrases that are almost always ironic in the wrong place.

Ironic lines and what they mean
SaidMeantWhy it works

Aferin sana, anahtarı yine unuttun.Not again: you've forgotten the key.Aferin (well done) is what you say to a child who did well
Elinize sağlık, tabağı da kırdınız.Thanks a lot: you broke the plate too.the cook's thanks (A2 unit 7), turned round

Zatıalileri teşrif ettiler!His Lordship has finally turned up.Ottoman formality aimed at a friend (C2 unit 3)

Irony on the page. Writing has no tone of voice, so writers mark irony. TDK's spelling guide puts an exclamation mark in brackets after a word meant ironically: Bu ne akıllı (!) bir karar. A narrator can also keep her distance from someone's claim with -mIş: Çok önemli bir işi varmış! (C2 unit 2).

Understatement and exaggeration. Saying less than you mean is everyday Turkish. Fena değil (not bad) can mean very good, and Hiç fena değil! certainly does; Hava biraz serin (it's a little cool) can mean it is freezing; Bir iki kitap okudum (I've read a book or two) can be an expert's modesty. Exaggeration (abartma) plays the other way: Bin kere söyledim!, Ölesiye yorgunum (C2 unit 2).

Teasing. Takılmak with the dative means to tease someone in a friendly way: Sana takılıyorum (I'm only teasing you). Dalga geçmek with -lA is to make fun of, more casual and less kind; laf sokmak is to slip in a pointed remark, a dig; iğnelemek, literally to pin, is to needle someone with hidden barbs. If a friend looks hurt, Şaka şaka! or Alınma, takılıyorum (don't take offence, I'm teasing) smooths it over.

Examples
  • Erken geldin, hayırdır?You're early, what's up? (to someone very late)Hayırdır (C1 unit 8) adds mock concern.
  • Hiç fena değil, eline sağlık!Not bad at all, it's delicious!Understatement as praise.
  • Sana takılıyorum, alınma.I'm only teasing you, don't take offence.
  • Toplantıda bana laf soktu.He made a dig at me in the meeting.
  • Bu ne akıllı (!) bir karar.What a clever (!) decision.The bracketed exclamation mark signals irony in writing.
Culture

Turkish humour teases the people one is closest to: friends, siblings, colleagues of the same age. The more formal the relationship, the less irony. With a teacher, a boss or an older stranger, keep to plain politeness (B2 unit 9).

Watch out

Alınmak has two senses: to take offence (Alındın mı?, did you take that badly?) and, as the passive of almak, to be taken or bought (Biletler alındı). Context decides, but with a person as the subject it is usually the first.

Summary

  1. Homonyms (sesteş) share sound and spelling: yüz, gül, yaz, at and düş each have a verb twin, because the imperative is the bare stem.
  2. Suffixes usually tell the twins apart (gülü, gülüyor), but some forms stay ambiguous, yazın and atın among them.
  3. Cinas puts homonyms in the rhyme: the kesik mani repeats its short first line with new meanings (bağlar: she ties, vineyards).
  4. A tekerleme is a tale formula or a tongue twister; TDK's precise word for the tongue twister is yanıltmaç.
  5. A fıkra has a setup, a turn and a one-line punchline; traditional ones are told in -mIş, modern jokes often in the present.
  6. Irony says the reverse (Erken geldin!); in writing it can be marked with (!) or with a distancing -mIş.
  7. Understatement (Fena değil) is everyday; teasing ranges from friendly takılmak to the sharper laf sokmak and iğnelemek.
